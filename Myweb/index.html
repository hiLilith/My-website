Weekly Reflections

Week 02Creating My Portfolio Website+

During the workshop held in the second week, I learned two methods for creating my own website. The first was CampusPress, the university's website platform, which involved a relatively straightforward process; the second was Visual Studio Code, which allows you to create your own web pages using the provided basic code and offers greater flexibility.

I opted for the second method because I wanted to try building a portfolio website with greater freedom using code.

The main task this time was to create a basic web page cover, including some of my personal details. In class, we mainly learnt how to create two files—index.html and a CSS file—and save them in a single folder. We also edited the text on the website based on the provided starter code.

The CSS file contained code to stylise the website, such as fonts, colours, typography, spacing and page layout.

Throughout this step-by-step learning process, I primarily gained practical experience of the process of building a website from scratch.

Furthermore, as discussed by Gillespie, websites digitise information from the real world through indexes and data, with almost all public activities being extensively recorded and archived (2014, p. 170).

This reveals that users' actions whilst browsing websites are stored, and that there are differing interpretations regarding data collection and the boundaries of the public sphere.

Week 03Web Scraping+

BBC iPlayer Data Collection

During the third week's workshop, I was introduced to web scraping and learned how to use the Web Scraper extension to collect information from BBC iPlayer and export it into a CSV file. This helped me understand how information presented on websites can be transformed into data for further analysis.

However, I noticed that the exported data was not always well-organised or complete. Some smaller sections of the webpage were difficult to capture accurately, which made the collected information appear somewhat messy. Compared with traditional paper documents, digital information is often organised in ways that make automated collection easier, but it is not necessarily as structured as an Excel spreadsheet.

This experience reminded me of Venturini and Rogers (2025, p. 81), who explain that digital information is organised according to the needs of its creators and platforms rather than those of academic researchers. Their argument helped me understand why information that appears organised on a website may not be directly suitable for research purposes.

Although web scraping can make data collection more efficient, I realised that automated tools cannot completely replace researchers' judgement. Researchers still need to examine the accuracy and completeness of collected data. As this workshop was mainly an introduction to the software, I hope to develop a better understanding of how to collect and organise more complex digital data in the future.

Reference

Venturini, T. and Rogers, R. (2025) Digital Methods: A Short Introduction. Cambridge: Polity Press. Chapter 6, 'Scraping Documents and Interfaces'.

Week 04Data and Data Analysis+

During this week's workshop, our group created a dataset to explore university students' use of generative AI and their attitudes towards it. As AI continues to develop rapidly, we wanted to understand how students experience and respond to these technologies.

We chose Reddit because of its large user base and opportunities for open discussion. Its voting system also allowed us to observe user interactions. To capture different perspectives, we developed detailed categories, including positive, negative, neutral and mixed attitudes.

However, while collecting 20 posts, I noticed that negative opinions appeared more frequently. This made me question how representative our data was. People who have used AI or hold strong opinions may be more willing to share their experiences, while students who do not use AI or rarely participate in online discussions may be overlooked.

This connects to D'Ignazio and Klein's (2020) What Gets Counted Counts, which highlights the relationship between data, classification and power. I realised that data is not entirely neutral. Reddit's community categories and voting mechanisms can influence which discussions become visible, while our own coding decisions also shape how different opinions are represented. Consequently, some voices may receive greater attention while others remain underrepresented.

Reflecting on this experience, I recognised the limitations of our small sample and the lack of a clear time frame. If I repeated this research, I would establish a more specific time range, expand the dataset and consider additional sources to capture a wider range of perspectives.

Reference

D'Ignazio, C. and Klein, L.F. (2020) 'What Gets Counted Counts'. In: Data Feminism. Cambridge: MIT Press, pp. 97–123.

Week 05Data Visualisation+

During this week's workshop, we continued working with the dataset collected in Week 4 and learned how to use Excel to create data visualisations, including bar charts and pie charts. I found this particularly useful because when a spreadsheet contains a large amount of information, it can be difficult to identify patterns simply by looking at numbers. Using different charts, colour contrasts and percentage labels makes the results more accessible and easier for audiences to understand. For example, we could visually present students' AI usage and attitudes, allowing viewers to recognise differences between categories more quickly.

This experience connects to Feigenbaum and Alamalhodaei's (2020) Visual Data Storytelling, which encouraged me to think critically about how data is organised and communicated through visual representations.

However, I also realised that visualisation can make data appear more convincing while potentially hiding its limitations. Although charts clearly present percentages, audiences may overlook the fact that our findings were based on only 20 Reddit posts, which cannot represent all university students.

More importantly, I began to recognise the relationship between data visualisation and power. The process of deciding what gets recorded, categorised and displayed is not entirely neutral. Researchers have the power to highlight certain information, while other perspectives may remain invisible. Therefore, I believe effective data visualisation should not only communicate findings clearly but also encourage audiences to recognise the limitations behind the data.

Reference

Feigenbaum, A. and Alamalhodaei, A. (2020) 'Visual Data Storytelling'. In: The Data Storytelling Workbook. Routledge.

Week 06Identity, Algorithms and Data+

Week 6 Reflection: Identity, Algorithms and Data

During this week's workshop, I explored how algorithms construct digital identities and how data classification may influence our understanding of ourselves and others.

In the first task, I accessed Instagram's Meta Account Centre to examine the categories associated with my account and the advertisers that included me in their audiences. Meta classified me through categories such as mobile network users, potential device changes, recently detected devices and Wi-Fi usage (figure 1). What concerned me most was not whether these classifications were accurate, but how ordinary personal behaviours could be recorded and interpreted as potential consumer needs.

I consider activities such as changing devices or moving between geographical locations to be part of my personal privacy. However, browsing activities, device information and potentially location-related data can become part of how platforms algorithmically construct our identities. Cheney-Lippold (2017) discusses this unsettling but increasingly common situation in which our identities are shaped through data and algorithmic classification (p.3). On the following page, he highlights how low data storage costs allow information about our everyday lives to accumulate continuously (Cheney-Lippold, 2017, p.4). This made me realise how personal activities can gradually become part of a much larger system of data collection, classification and commercial prediction.

I also found several advertisers associated with my account, including Monzo, Wise, Uber and OpenAI (figure 2). When I examined OpenAI's details, Meta indicated that the advertiser had uploaded or used a list to reach me (figure 3). Although this does not explain exactly which information was used, it made me question how data can be connected across different services for advertising purposes. Meta allows users to remove certain categories, but I consider this a limited and rather superficial form of control. These visible categories represent only a small part of a much broader system, and removing one label does not necessarily explain or control how the underlying information is processed.

In the second task, I followed Sumpter's (2018) classification framework to manually collect data from 32 Instagram friends. I examined their 15 most recent posts, creating a dataset of 480 posts, and recorded the classifications in the Week 6 Sumpter's Matrix using the 13 provided categories. Each post was assigned one category and one point, with a total of 15 points for each person.

However, I found that many posts could reasonably belong to multiple categories, while the framework only permitted one classification. For example, Outdoors and Lifestyle frequently overlapped. I also placed many gaming-related posts under Lifestyle because there was no separate gaming category. Ultimately, these decisions depended on my own interpretation. This made me realise that, as the person collecting and classifying the data, I also held the power to determine how other people's online identities were represented.

Sumpter (2018) discusses the question of whether algorithms can help overcome the biases involved in human decision-making (p.75). However, my experience made me question whether algorithmic results can truly be objective or accurately reflect reality. Even if an algorithm follows consistent rules, the data and categories used may already contain subjective decisions and limitations.

For example, one of my friends mainly posts on Instagram when going out, while rarely sharing ordinary daily activities. Based on the collected data, this person might appear to have an outdoors-focused lifestyle, but this does not necessarily reflect their actual daily life. The dataset represents what they choose to share, rather than their complete identity.

Reflecting on both tasks, I realised that digital identities are shaped not only by algorithms, but also by what data are collected, what users choose to share, and who has the power to classify and interpret that information. Classification may simplify complex human behaviours into seemingly objective numbers, while overlooking the contexts and experiences behind them.

If the data only captures selected fragments of our lives, how far can algorithms truly understand who we are?

References

Cheney-Lippold, J. (2017). Introduction. In: We Are Data: Algorithms and the Making of Our Digital Selves. New York: NYU Press, pp. 1–32.

Sumpter, D. (2018). Chapter 3: The Principal Components of Friendship. In: Outnumbered: From Facebook and Google to Fake News and Filter-Bubbles – The Algorithms That Control Our Lives. London: Bloomsbury.

Week 07The Shaky Line — Identity, Relationships and Human Imperfection+

Week 7 Reflection: The Shaky Line — Identity, Relationships and Human Imperfection

This week, I watched We Were Here (Bhasin, 2026), a short film that made me reflect on human identity, relationships and the growing distance between generations in a rapidly developing technological world.

What interested me most was the relationship between the father and son. The son works with data in an AI company and believes that AI knows everything, while his father protests against robots replacing human labour. Their disagreement reflects not only a generational gap, but also their different experiences and understandings of technological development. When technology develops so quickly that some people struggle to keep up, how can they prove that their existence still matters? I think the father's resistance does not simply come from rejecting technology, but from a fear of becoming unnecessary in a world where machines can increasingly replace human abilities.

One moment that particularly stayed with me was the comparison between a washing machine and a mother. A washing machine can free our hands from repetitive labour and prevent the physical damage caused by washing clothes, but it cannot replace a mother or the care and emotions behind her actions. Similarly, giving a tree two different names shows how an ordinary object can carry different meanings through people's personal experiences and relationships. These moments reminded me that our identities are not simply defined by what we do, but also by our stories, memories, relationships and how we understand our own experiences. Machines may replace certain forms of labour, but can they truly understand the emotions behind these experiences?

Towards the end of the film, the son discovers that AI cannot even correctly identify a pizza. This challenges his earlier belief that AI knows everything and helps him begin to understand his father's fear and resistance. To me, this suggests that fear and rejection alone cannot help us understand one another. Although the father and son live in the same world, their different experiences of technology create misunderstandings between them. I interpreted the idea of peace at the end of the film as more than coexistence between humans and machines. It also represents the possibility of rebuilding understanding between people. Technological progress may improve efficiency, but it should not make us overlook those who struggle to adapt or the value of genuine human relationships.

This also connects to our Week 7 workshop, where we reflected on Sumpter's Matrix and our experience of categorising friends' social media posts. During the experiment, I found that categories such as Outdoors and Lifestyle often overlapped, making it difficult to assign a post to only one category. However, beyond the limitations of classification, I began to question whether these numerical results could truly represent a person's identity.

For example, some of my friends mainly share pictures of their pets on social media. The data might therefore suggest that animals are an important part of their identities, but this does not mean their lives only revolve around pets. They may have other interests, relationships and experiences that they simply choose not to share online. Even though I know some of these people personally, the data I collected cannot fully reflect who they are in real life. These cold numerical results may represent fragments of a person's life, but they are more accurately representations constructed through algorithms and classification. Interacting with someone in real life is different from understanding them through their online profile. Their personal stories, emotions and relationships cannot always fit into predefined categories.

This made me reflect further on whether our attitudes towards AI and data might also influence our understanding of ourselves and others. Data does not simply record our behaviour; it may also shape what we see, how we act and what we choose to share. For example, if an algorithm repeatedly identifies someone through their interest in pets, it may continue recommending similar content, reinforcing only one part of their identity. Over time, these recommendations might even influence what they choose to post. In this sense, algorithms do not merely represent our identities, but may also participate in shaping them. If we increasingly rely on data to understand people, will we gradually overlook the parts of their lives that are not recorded?

The most meaningful symbol for me was the shaky line. A machine can draw a perfectly straight line, while a human's line shakes because they are breathing. I understood this imperfection as a trace of human existence. Our lives are shaped by emotions, memories, misunderstandings and the friction of everyday experiences. These imperfections are not necessarily weaknesses, but part of what makes us human. Just as the father and son cannot immediately understand each other, human understanding is not always perfect. However, through communication, empathy and shared experiences, we can gradually learn to understand one another.

Through this week's film and workshop, I realised that reflective digital practice should not only focus on how efficiently we collect, classify and analyse data, but also on what these processes might leave behind. Technology can improve our lives and replace certain forms of labour, but it cannot simply replace our identities or the genuine emotions and relationships that give meaning to our experiences. Rather than completely rejecting AI or blindly trusting it, I think we need to reflect on how we use technology and how our understanding of data may influence our relationships with others and with ourselves.

If algorithms increasingly shape how we see ourselves and others, are we discovering who we truly are, or gradually becoming who our data suggests we should be?

Reference

Bhasin, P. 2026. We Were Here, The New Yorker, 29th October 2026. Available at: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EMSQtqUIlXY
